# Decision Brief

Create a decision artifact that helps a person choose, not a long list that hands the work back to them.

## Workflow
1. **Frame the decision.** Write the exact choice, decision owner, deadline, non-negotiables, and consequences of delay. Separate the decision from related questions.
2. **Generate a small option set.** Usually include: preferred candidate, credible alternative, keep the status quo, and “do a smaller test” if uncertainty is high. Do not invent implausible straw options.
3. **Set criteria before scoring.** Use outcomes the decision owner cares about: cost, reliability, time, risk, reversibility, maintainability, accessibility, privacy, or strategic fit. Distinguish hard constraints from preferences.
4. **Gather comparable evidence.** Use the same assumptions, timeframe, units, and scope across options. Cite current or niche facts. Mark unknowns rather than filling empty cells with guesses.
5. **Score transparently when useful.** Use a simple ordinal scale and define what each score means. Weight criteria only if the weights have a clear rationale. Treat totals as a thinking aid, not mathematical truth.
6. **Name the trade-offs.** State what the recommended option optimizes, what it sacrifices, what could go wrong, and whose needs it may not fit.
7. **Stress-test the recommendation.** Ask what would make the second-best option win, which assumption is most fragile, and what failure would be hardest to reverse.
8. **Choose the smallest uncertainty-reducing experiment.** When stakes are material and evidence is weak, propose a time-boxed test with a success threshold.
9. **Define the decision and trigger to revisit.** Give a recommendation, confidence level, owner, next action, and explicit signals that should reopen the decision.

## Output format
- **Decision needed** and deadline
- **Recommendation** in one sentence
- **Options compared** and criteria
- **Evidence and assumptions**
- **Risks / trade-offs**
- **What would change the recommendation**
- **Next action, owner, date**

## Quality bar
A reader should understand not only what you recommend, but why it wins, what it costs, and what evidence would make you change your mind.
